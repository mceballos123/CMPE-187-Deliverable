PROMPT:
What are the reasons SNMP usually uses UDP and not TCP as its transport?

ANSWER:
SNMP (Simple Network Management Protocol) defaults to UDP (typically ports 161 for queries/responses and 162 for traps/informs) rather than TCP primarily because network management needs to stay lightweight, fast, and operational even when the network itself is degraded.
1. Low Overhead and Minimal Resource Footprint
No handshake or state tracking: UDP is connectionless, avoiding the 3-way TCP handshake, connection teardown, sequence number tracking, and retransmission timers.
Lightweight for embedded devices: Routers, switches, printers, UPS units, and IoT hardware often have constrained CPU and memory. Avoiding TCP connection state keeps agent implementations small and predictable.
2. Operates During Network Congestion or Failure
Monitoring when things break: Network management traffic is most critical during link flapping, packet loss, congestion, or device reboots.
Avoiding TCP stalls: TCP's congestion control, backoffs, and head-of-line blocking can delay or stall management messages right when an administrator needs visibility into what is failing.
3. Scales Better Across Large Fleets
Many managed nodes: A single Network Management System (NMS) may poll hundreds or thousands of devices periodically.
No persistent sockets required: With UDP, a manager can send lightweight requests and receive replies or asynchronous traps without maintaining thousands of open TCP connections or paying repeated connection-setup costs.
4. Fits SNMP's Request–Response and Trap Model
Short, discrete exchanges: Most SNMP operations are small, self-contained messages (GetRequest, GetNextRequest, GetBulkRequest, Response, Trap).
Application-level handling: SNMP handles retries, timeouts, and request IDs at the application layer—allowing the manager to decide whether to retry a timed-out poll or move on without waiting on TCP's transport-layer retransmission logic.
When TCP Is Used Instead
While UDP is the standard default, SNMP over TCP (also on ports 161/162) is supported in newer versions (such as SNMPv2c and SNMPv3) and can be useful when:
Large payloads (such as big GetBulk tables) are transferred over lossy links and reliable delivery without app-layer re-polling is desired.
Firewalls or NAT gateways filter UDP management traffic more strictly than TCP.
(Note: Because UDP does not guarantee delivery on its own, reliability in SNMP is handled through manager timeouts/retries for polls and Inform messages—which require an acknowledgment—rather than via TCP.)
