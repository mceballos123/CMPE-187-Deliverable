PROMPT:
Why does SNMP normally run over UDP instead of TCP?

ANSWER:
SNMP (Simple Network Management Protocol) normally runs over UDP (typically ports 161 for queries/responses and 162 for traps/informs) primarily because network monitoring favors low overhead, simplicity, and resilience during network trouble over connection-oriented delivery.
Why UDP Fits SNMP
Minimal overhead for frequent polling: Monitoring systems often poll hundreds or thousands of devices every few seconds or minutes for metrics like CPU load, interface counters, and errors. UDP avoids TCP's three-way handshake, connection state tracking, and ACK overhead for short request-response exchanges.
Works when links or devices are congested or failing: During outages, congestion, or packet loss—precisely when you want SNMP telemetry most—TCP connections can stall, time out, or struggle to establish while management stations try to reach degraded devices.
Simpler agent footprint: Early network gear (routers, switches, printers, UPS units) had tight CPU and memory budgets. Implementing a stateless UDP listener/responder is much lighter than maintaining per-client TCP state tables across many polling stations.
Built-in tolerance for lost polls: Most SNMP polling is periodic and idempotent—if a single GET or GETNEXT packet is dropped, the management station simply retries or waits for the next polling interval rather than needing stream-level retransmission.
Trade-Offs & Exceptions
Reliability is handled at the application/polling layer (or via INFORM): Because UDP does not guarantee delivery or ordering, SNMP relies on timeouts and retries by the polling manager, and SNMPv2c/v3 INFORM messages add application-level acknowledgments (unlike fire-and-forget TRAP messages).
SNMP over TCP does exist: Modern SNMP implementations and standards allow running SNMP over TCP (including secure transports like DTLS over UDP and TLS over TCP in SNMPv3) when traversing firewalls/NATs more predictably or transferring larger payloads is desired, even though UDP remains the default standard.
