PROMPT:
Why does SNMP normally run over UDP instead of TCP?

ANSWER:
SNMP (Simple Network Management Protocol) normally runs over UDP (typically ports 161 and 162) primarily because it was designed to be lightweight, low-overhead, and reliable even when the network itself is congested or failing.
1. Low Overhead for Frequent Polling
Network monitoring relies heavily on periodic polling—sending thousands of small GET requests across routers, switches, and servers to check interface counters, CPU usage, or memory. UDP avoids TCP's three-way handshake, connection state tracking, and acknowledgments on every exchange, keeping packet sizes small and CPU/memory load low on embedded network devices.
2. Works When TCP Might Struggle or Fail
Congestion and packet loss: During a network outage, link saturation, or broadcast storm, TCP's congestion control and retransmission timers can back off or stall just when you most need diagnostic visibility.
Connection state limits: Network devices often have limited resources and may handle large numbers of management agents or transient monitoring scripts. Avoiding long-lived TCP connection state reduces the risk of exhausting connection tables.
3. Stateless Request-Response & Fire-and-Forget Traps
Many SNMP operations are short, self-contained queries (GET, SET, GETNEXT, GETBULK) or asynchronous notifications (TRAPs). In SNMPv1/v2c, traps are "fire-and-forget" notifications sent from an agent to a management station without waiting for a response—fitting UDP's connectionless model naturally.
4. Reliability Is Handled at the Application Layer (When Needed)
Choosing UDP doesn't mean SNMP ignores lost packets:
Polling retries & timeouts: The management station (NMS) simply times out and resends a GET or SET request if no response arrives.
SNMPv2c/v3 INFORM messages: Unlike unacknowledged TRAPs, an INFORM requires the receiving manager to send back a response, letting the sender retry if it isn't acknowledged.
When Is SNMP Over TCP Used?
While UDP is the standard default, SNMP over TCP (RFC 3430) is occasionally used when traversing strict firewalls/NATs that block or mishandle UDP, or when transporting large payloads/bulk transfers where reliable stream delivery is preferred over connectionless simplicity.
